Why weight loss plateaus (even when you're doing everything right)

Dr. Ingmar Lindström, MD
March 3, 2026
8 min read

A plateau can feel uniquely discouraging: you change your eating, you move more, and the scale simply stops. For some people this happens while taking GLP‑1 medication (such as semaglutide); for others it happens with lifestyle changes alone.

In most cases, a plateau is not a sign that you're “broken” or that you’ve failed. It’s a predictable response of the body to weight loss.

Disclaimer: This article is for general information only and is not medical advice. If you have medical conditions, take prescription medications, are pregnant/trying to conceive, or have concerning symptoms, please discuss your situation with your clinician.

Why plateaus happen

1) Your energy needs drop as you lose weight

When your body is smaller, it usually needs fewer calories to maintain basic functions and daily movement. That alone can narrow the calorie deficit that drove early progress.

2) Metabolic adaptation (adaptive thermogenesis)

Many people also experience a reduction in energy expenditure that is larger than expected from weight loss alone. This is often called metabolic adaptation or adaptive thermogenesis. The size of the effect varies widely between individuals, and it can make continued loss—and long-term maintenance—harder.

3) Appetite signalling changes after weight loss

After weight loss, hormonal signals related to hunger and fullness often shift in a direction that increases appetite. Clinically, people often describe this as “food noise” returning, even when motivation is high.

4) Unnoticed movement changes (NEAT)

During dieting (and sometimes with fatigue), many people unconsciously move less throughout the day. This reduction in non‑exercise activity can meaningfully reduce total daily energy expenditure.

What to reassess (gently)

Re-check intake and portions—without turning it into punishment

A short period of tracking can be useful as data: portions can drift, “small extras” add up, and protein/fibre targets can be missed. If tracking increases anxiety or triggers disordered eating patterns, it’s better done with professional support.

Sleep and stress

Short sleep is associated with hormonal and behavioural changes linked to increased hunger and higher‑calorie food choices. Stress can also affect sleep, eating patterns, and consistency—effects vary from person to person.

Medications and medical conditions

A plateau is also a good moment to check for factors that can contribute to weight gain or make weight loss harder: untreated sleep apnoea, thyroid disease, depression, and certain medications (for example some antidepressants, antipsychotics, or steroids). This is not something to self‑diagnose—just a reminder that context matters.

When it makes sense to get help

If your plateau has lasted several weeks, or you feel stuck in an exhausting cycle of restriction and rebound, it may help to have a clinician review the full picture: symptoms, labs where appropriate, medications, nutrition, and activity.
