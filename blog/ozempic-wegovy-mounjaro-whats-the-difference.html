GLP‑1 / Incretin Medications

Ozempic, Wegovy, and Mounjaro — what’s the difference?

Dr. Ingmar Lindström, MD
March 3, 2026
9 min read

People often use these names interchangeably in casual conversation, but they are not identical. The differences that matter most are: what the medication contains, what it is approved for, and how it should be used safely.

Disclaimer: This is general information, not medical advice. These are prescription medications. Decisions about starting, stopping, or switching should be made with a clinician who can review your history, labs, and other medications.

What they have in common

Ozempic and Wegovy are semaglutide products (a GLP‑1 receptor agonist). Mounjaro is tirzepatide (a dual GIP/GLP‑1 agonist). These medications generally reduce energy intake by increasing satiety and reducing appetite, and they can improve metabolic health markers in appropriate patients.

Ozempic (semaglutide)

Ozempic is primarily approved for type 2 diabetes management in many regions. Weight loss can occur as a secondary benefit. In clinical practice, some clinicians may prescribe semaglutide off‑label for weight management when appropriate—this should be an individualized, clinician‑supervised decision.

Wegovy (semaglutide)

Wegovy contains the same active ingredient as Ozempic (semaglutide) but is approved as a weight‑management medication. The pivotal STEP‑1 trial showed clinically meaningful average weight loss compared with placebo when combined with lifestyle intervention.

Mounjaro (tirzepatide)

Mounjaro contains tirzepatide and targets two incretin pathways (GIP and GLP‑1). In trials (SURMOUNT‑1), tirzepatide produced large average weight loss compared with placebo. Regulatory status and brand naming for the weight‑management indication varies by country.

Side effects (what most people notice)

The most common side effects across these medicines are gastrointestinal—especially during dose escalation:

  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Constipation or diarrhoea
  • Reduced appetite

Key safety warnings (high level)

Seek urgent medical care if you have severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down, signs of dehydration, or jaundice/fever with right‑upper‑abdominal pain.

Important cautions that commonly come up in clinic include:

  • Pregnancy / trying to conceive / breastfeeding: generally not recommended—discuss with your clinician.
  • Personal/family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or MEN2: contraindications per product labeling.
  • Gallbladder disease: rapid weight loss and GLP‑1 therapies can be associated with gallstones in some patients.
  • Diabetes combinations: hypoglycaemia risk increases when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas—dose adjustments may be needed.
  • Severe gastrointestinal disease / gastroparesis: caution due to effects on gastric emptying.
  • Procedures/anesthesia: delayed gastric emptying may matter—inform your treating team.

What to ask before you start (or switch)

  • Am I a good candidate based on my BMI, medical history, and contraindications?
  • What is our monitoring plan (weight, symptoms, blood pressure, labs where appropriate)?
  • What support is in place for nutrition, activity, and long‑term maintenance?
  • What is the plan if I need to stop—especially to reduce rebound risk?
