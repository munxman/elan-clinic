Weight Management

You’re not lazy. You may need a different approach.

Dr. Ingmar Lindström, MD
March 3, 2026
7 min read

If you’ve tried “everything” and still can’t lose weight, it’s very common to assume the problem is character: not enough discipline, not enough motivation, not enough willpower.

In clinical practice, that story is often incomplete. Body weight is influenced by biology, environment, sleep, stress, medications, and lived experience—not just intentions.

Disclaimer: This article is for general information only and is not medical advice. If you have medical conditions, take prescription medications, or have a history of an eating disorder, please discuss weight-related goals with an appropriately qualified clinician.

What “tried everything” usually means

Most people have worked through a long list of approaches: calorie cutting, low‑carb, low‑fat, intermittent fasting, meal replacements, intense exercise phases—often followed by rebound.

Energy balance still matters. But in real life, the body adapts: hunger signals can increase, energy expenditure can decrease, and adherence becomes harder over time. That doesn’t make the effort meaningless—it means the system is more complex than “just try harder.”

Why shame tends to backfire

Weight stigma and self‑blame are associated with worse health behaviours and—across multiple studies—worse long‑term outcomes. Shame can also increase avoidance of care, which delays diagnosis of treatable contributors (sleep apnoea, depression, hypothyroidism, medication effects).

When a medical review is especially useful

Consider discussing your situation with a clinician if any of these apply:

  • Repeated cycles of weight loss and regain that leave you feeling exhausted
  • Persistent hunger that feels out of proportion to what you eat
  • Symptoms suggesting sleep apnoea (snoring, daytime sleepiness), thyroid disease, or depression
  • Medications that may contribute to weight gain (for example some antidepressants, antipsychotics, or steroids)
  • Your relationship with food feels distressed, obsessive, or out of control

The goal of a review isn’t to “find a perfect diet.” It’s to map the constraints: biology, schedule, mental health, social environment, and treatment options.

A different approach doesn’t have to mean “more intense”

For some people, the most effective next step is structured behavioural support. For others, it’s addressing sleep, stress, or a medical condition. For others, it may include evidence‑based pharmacotherapy—always with clinician oversight.
