Starting GLP-1 Therapy: What Your Doctor Evaluates First: A Physician's Guide

By Dr. Ingmar Lindström, MD Élan Clinic · Tallinn Last reviewed: March 2026
Medical Disclaimer This article is for education only and does not provide individual medical advice. GLP-1 medications are prescription treatments and should be started, adjusted, or stopped only with a licensed prescriber who knows your medical history. If you have severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, dehydration, fainting, or signs of an allergic reaction, seek urgent medical care.

Why this guide exists (and who it’s for)

GLP-1 medications (like semaglutide and tirzepatide) are now mainstream — and access is increasingly messy. In real life, people don’t only choose between “start” and “don’t start.” They end up navigating:

  • Insurance and prior authorisation hurdles (especially in the U.S.)
  • Price shocks and supply disruptions
  • Online clinics and mail-order pharmacies
  • Compounded products and “too good to be true” offers

When access becomes hard, risk increases — because people start improvising. This article is a physician’s safety checklist: how to get GLP-1 medication through a safer pathway, what to verify, and what to avoid.

What this is (and isn’t) This is not a guide to “where to buy Ozempic cheaply.” It’s a framework for reducing avoidable harm: dosing errors, warm shipments, fraudulent products, and unsafe self-titration.

Step 1: Start with the safest access pathway

The safest pathway is boring — and that’s the point.

  1. Medical evaluation with a licensed clinician
  2. Prescription for an approved GLP-1 medication when appropriate
  3. Dispensing by a licensed pharmacy with correct storage, labelling, and counselling
  4. Structured follow-up for dose titration, side effects, and outcomes

Step 2: Understand why “access” has become harder

Access barriers vary by country, but two patterns are consistent: cost is high, and administration is heavy.

Administrative friction (U.S. example)

In the U.S., coverage often depends on diagnosis and plan policy. Secondary reporting of a 2025 JAMA analysis described near-universal prior authorisation requirements for GLP-1 medications in Medicare Part D plans in 2025, along with higher cost-sharing. Whether you live in the U.S. or not, the practical lesson is the same: when legitimate access becomes difficult, people seek alternatives — and those alternatives can be riskier.

Policy coverage gaps (U.S. example)

Policy analyses from Penn LDI have highlighted how coverage decisions can change quickly and how many U.S. Medicaid programs historically did not cover GLP-1s for adult weight loss (and often required prior authorisation where they did). That volatility contributes to stop-start treatment and risky workarounds.

Key point If you are being pushed toward a riskier product or a non-medical route because “there’s no other way,” pause. There are often safer alternatives (different medication, slower titration, structured discontinuation, or a medically supervised plan) that reduce harm even when cost and supply are constraints.

Step 3: If you’re using an online clinic, verify the basics

Online prescribing can be legitimate — but it also creates an easy path for low-quality services to scale.

Green flags (good signs)

  • Real medical intake (history, medications, contraindications, baseline metrics)
  • Clear clinician identity and licensure
  • Clear pharmacy identity, licensure, and contact details
  • Written titration plan and what-to-do-if guidance
  • Follow-up plan (not a one-off transaction)

Red flags (walk away)

  • No real medical history taken
  • No clinician contact (only chatbots or sales staff)
  • Medication source unclear (“our partner pharmacy” with no name)
  • Pressure tactics (limited-time offers, aggressive upsells)
  • Payment methods that reduce traceability (wire/crypto only)

Step 4: Be especially cautious with compounded GLP-1 products

This is where most preventable harm happens. The U.S. FDA has published multiple warnings relevant to compounded GLP-1 products (even if you live outside the U.S., the safety mechanisms are universal).

Risk #1: Dosing errors from multi-dose vials

FDA has alerted clinicians, compounders, and patients about dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide, including reports of adverse events, some requiring hospitalisation, that may be related to overdoses from dosing mistakes. A common mechanism is confusion when patients must draw medication from a vial and convert between mg, mL, and “units”, with variable concentrations across compounders.

Safety rule: never inject a dose you can’t express clearly You (and your prescriber) should be able to state your dose in milligrams (mg), and the vial concentration in mg per mL. If the only instruction you’re given is “inject X units,” without a clear mg conversion, you are in a high-risk situation.

Risk #2: Warm shipments and storage uncertainty

FDA has also described complaints about compounded GLP-1 products arriving warm or with inadequate ice packs. Temperature excursions can degrade peptides and create uncertainty about potency. FDA’s consumer-facing guidance includes a simple recommendation: patients should not use injectable GLP-1 products that arrive warm.

Risk #3: Fraudulent labelling / counterfeit risk

FDA notes reports of compounded products with false label information, including situations where the listed compounding pharmacy did not exist or did not manufacture the product.

Risk #4: “Salt forms” of semaglutide

FDA has warned that some compounded products use semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate. FDA states these are different active ingredients than in approved medicines and that it lacks information that they share the same chemical and pharmacologic properties. FDA also notes it is not aware of a lawful basis for their use in compounding.

If you remember only one “compounding” red flag If a product is described as semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate, treat that as a serious warning sign and discuss alternatives with a licensed clinician.

Step 5: A practical “safe access” checklist

Use this checklist before starting any GLP-1 medication sourced outside a familiar local pharmacy chain.

Prescription and labelling

  • Do you have a real prescription in your name?
  • Does the label include your name, the prescriber, the pharmacy, and dosing instructions?
  • Is the dose written in mg (not only “units”)?

Pharmacy verification

  • Can you verify the pharmacy is licensed in your jurisdiction?
  • Is there a real phone number where a pharmacist can counsel you?

Shipping and storage

  • Did the shipment arrive with appropriate cold packaging?
  • Did it arrive warm or delayed? If yes: do not inject until clarified with the dispensing pharmacy/manufacturer.

Dosing safety (where people get hurt)

  • Can you describe your dose in mg and your injection volume in mL?
  • Do you have the correct syringe size and clear measuring instructions?
  • Do you have a conservative titration plan (not rapid escalation)?

Step 6: What to do when cost forces discontinuation

This is one of the most common pain points: “I want to continue, but I can’t afford it.” Two mistakes happen here:

  1. Abrupt stopping without a maintenance plan
  2. Risky sourcing from unknown channels

Safer next steps:

  • Discuss dose optimisation (some people can maintain at a lower dose; this is individual)
  • Plan a structured discontinuation strategy (see: How to taper off GLP-1 safely)
  • Build a “maintenance architecture” (protein, resistance training, sleep, follow-up cadence)
  • Reassess alternatives with your clinician (other medications, comorbidity management, behaviour and nutrition supports)
Important reframe If GLP-1 therapy meaningfully improves your health, stopping solely due to cost is a system failure — not a personal failure. But you still need a safe off-ramp.

Final word

GLP-1 access should not require detective work. But until systems catch up, people will keep getting pushed toward riskier routes. Use this guide to reduce avoidable harm: verify the source, respect dosing math, don’t accept warm shipments, and don’t accelerate titration because a seller says it’s “fine.”
