You have been prescribed a GLP-1 receptor agonist. This is a significant, evidence-based step in your metabolic health journey. Below is everything you need to know to start safely.

What you've been prescribed is a medication that mimics a natural gut hormone (GLP-1) to reduce appetite, slow digestion, and improve blood sugar regulation. Common medications in this class include semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro).

DETAILED GUIDE

Pages 2-10

1. What Are GLP-1 Medications?

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonists are a class of medications originally developed to treat type 2 diabetes. Over the past decade, clinical research has demonstrated that they are among the most effective pharmacological tools available for weight management and metabolic health.

These medications mimic GLP-1, a hormone your gut naturally releases after eating. By activating GLP-1 receptors throughout the body - particularly in the brain, stomach, and pancreas - they produce multiple beneficial effects simultaneously.

The Medications in This Class

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist available in two formulations:

  • Ozempic - licensed for type 2 diabetes management; widely used off-label for weight management
  • Wegovy - the higher-dose formulation licensed specifically for chronic weight management

Tirzepatide is a dual-action medication that activates both GLP-1 and GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) receptors:

  • Mounjaro - licensed for type 2 diabetes; increasingly available for weight management
  • The dual mechanism appears to produce somewhat greater weight loss compared to semaglutide in head-to-head trials

Your physician has selected the specific medication and dose appropriate for your clinical profile. The principles in this guide apply to both.

2. How They Work - Plain Language

Think of your body's hunger system as a thermostat that has been set too high. GLP-1 medications work by resetting that thermostat through several simultaneous mechanisms:

In the brain: GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus and brainstem receive stronger signals of fullness and satiety. You feel satisfied with less food. Food cravings - particularly for high-fat, high-sugar foods - diminish for most patients.

In the stomach: These medications slow gastric emptying, meaning food moves more slowly from your stomach into your small intestine. This prolongs the feeling of fullness after eating and flattens blood sugar spikes.

In the pancreas: GLP-1 medications stimulate insulin release in a glucose-dependent manner (only when blood sugar is elevated), while suppressing glucagon (the hormone that raises blood sugar). This improves blood sugar regulation without the hypoglycaemia risk seen with some older diabetes medications when GLP-1 therapy is used alone. However, when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas, low blood sugar can still occur.

In the liver: Reduced glucagon signalling decreases the liver's tendency to release stored glucose inappropriately, further supporting metabolic control.

The result is a lower caloric intake driven by genuine physiological changes - not willpower. This is why GLP-1 medications work when diets alone have not. Obesity is a biological condition with strong hormonal drivers; these medications address those drivers directly.

3. Expected Benefits

Weight Loss Timeline

Weight loss with GLP-1 medications is gradual and progressive. The following reflects typical clinical outcomes; individual results vary based on diet, exercise, starting weight, and medication dose.

  • Weeks 1-4: Little to no visible weight loss. Your body is adapting to the medication. Side effects are most prominent in this phase.
  • Months 1-3: Most patients begin to see meaningful weight loss, typically 2-5% of starting body weight. Appetite suppression becomes noticeable.
  • Months 3-6: Weight loss accelerates as dose increases. Many patients reach 8-12% body weight reduction.
  • Months 6-12: Continued loss toward plateau. Clinical trials with semaglutide (STEP trials) showed approximately 15% average body weight loss at 68 weeks. Tirzepatide trials (SURMOUNT) demonstrated up to 20-22% in some cohorts.
  • Beyond 12 months: The medication supports weight maintenance. This phase requires attention to lifestyle consolidation.

Metabolic Improvements

Beyond weight loss, GLP-1 medications produce measurable improvements in:

  • Blood sugar regulation: HbA1c reductions of 1-2% are typical in patients with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes
  • Blood pressure: Modest reductions in systolic blood pressure (average 3-5 mmHg in trials)
  • Lipid profile: Reductions in triglycerides; variable effects on LDL and HDL
  • Cardiovascular risk: Semaglutide has demonstrated statistically significant cardiovascular risk reduction in overweight/obese patients with established cardiovascular disease (SELECT trial, 2023: 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events in this specific high-risk group). Whether this benefit extends to lower-risk patients is under active investigation
  • Liver health: Emerging evidence supports improvement in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD/NASH)
  • Inflammatory markers: CRP and other inflammatory markers tend to decrease with significant weight loss

These metabolic benefits begin before major weight loss is achieved and are an important part of why this medication has been prescribed.

4. Common Side Effects and How to Manage Them

The majority of side effects from GLP-1 medications are gastrointestinal, predictable, and manageable. They are most pronounced during dose escalation and typically diminish over time.

Nausea

Nausea is the most common side effect, affecting approximately 40-50% of patients at some point during treatment.

Why it happens: Slowed gastric emptying and central GLP-1 receptor activation in the brainstem.

Management:

  • Eat smaller, more frequent meals
  • Avoid fatty, fried, or spicy foods during the adjustment period
  • Eat slowly and stop eating before you feel full
  • Avoid lying down immediately after eating
  • Stay well hydrated - sipping cold water or ginger tea can help
  • Time your injection on a day when you can take it easy (many patients prefer Friday evening if weekends are quieter)
  • Over-the-counter antiemetics (e.g., dimenhydrinate) can be used short-term - ask your pharmacist

Nausea that prevents you from eating or drinking requires medical attention. Contact the clinic.

Constipation

Affects approximately 20-30% of patients. Slowed gut motility is the primary cause.

Management:

  • Increase fluid intake to at least 2.0-2.5 litres of water daily
  • Increase dietary fibre gradually (vegetables, legumes, psyllium husk)
  • Light physical activity - even walking - supports gut motility
  • Over-the-counter osmotic laxatives (e.g., macrogol/PEG) are safe and effective if needed
  • Discuss with your physician if constipation is severe or persistent

Diarrhoea

Less common than constipation but can occur, particularly early in treatment.

Management:

  • Usually self-limiting within days
  • Stay hydrated
  • Avoid high-fat meals
  • Contact the clinic if severe or lasting more than 48 hours

Vomiting

Occasional vomiting is manageable. Persistent vomiting that prevents fluid intake is a red flag.

Management:

  • Sip fluids slowly and frequently
  • Temporarily reduce food intake to bland, easily tolerated items
  • If you cannot keep fluids down for more than 12 hours, contact the clinic

Heart Rate (Pulse)

A small increase in resting heart rate (often around 2-5 beats per minute) is a recognised effect of GLP-1 medications and is not typically cause for concern.

Contact the clinic if you have a sustained resting heart rate above 100 bpm that is new for you, or if you have palpitations, chest discomfort, dizziness, or fainting.

Fatigue

Some patients experience fatigue, particularly during the initial weeks.

Management:

  • Ensure adequate protein and caloric intake - under-eating on GLP-1 medications is common and contributes to fatigue
  • Maintain hydration
  • Prioritise sleep

Injection Site Reactions

Mild redness, bruising, or itching at the injection site is normal.

Management:

  • Rotate injection sites consistently (see Section 6)
  • Allow the medication to reach room temperature before injecting
  • Do not inject into areas with active skin irritation

5. Serious Risks and Red Flags

GLP-1 medications have a strong safety profile in clinical use. However, certain symptoms require prompt medical evaluation.

Contact the Clinic Immediately If You Experience:

Severe abdominal pain - particularly pain that is persistent, severe, or radiates to the back. This may indicate pancreatitis, a rare but serious complication. Do not wait to see if it improves.

Severe pain in the upper right abdomen - especially after eating fatty foods, or if accompanied by fever, nausea, or jaundice (yellowing of the skin/eyes). This may indicate gallstones or gallbladder inflammation, a recognised complication during rapid weight loss and GLP-1 treatment.

Persistent vomiting - if you cannot keep fluids down for more than 12 hours, you risk dehydration and electrolyte imbalance. Seek medical attention.

Symptoms of severe allergic reaction - including swelling of the face, lips, or throat; widespread rash or hives; or difficulty breathing. Call 112 immediately.

Unexplained rapid heart rate - a sustained resting heart rate above 100 bpm that is new or unusual for you warrants evaluation.

Vision changes - rare cases of worsening diabetic eye disease (retinopathy) have been reported with rapid glucose improvement in diabetic patients. Any new vision changes should be reported.

Symptoms of low blood sugar (if you are also taking insulin or a sulfonylurea) - shakiness, sweating, confusion, rapid heartbeat. GLP-1 medications alone do not usually cause hypoglycaemia, but combinations with other glucose-lowering medications can.

If you are on insulin: your insulin dose will often need to be reduced when starting a GLP-1 medication. This should be adjusted by your physician before or at initiation - do not wait for hypoglycaemic symptoms to appear.

Signs of dehydration - dark urine, minimal urine output, severe dizziness, or confusion. This can develop quickly if nausea and vomiting are present alongside reduced oral intake.

Important Contraindications to Discuss With Your Physician

  • Personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2 (MEN2) - GLP-1 medications carry a theoretical risk based on animal data; their use in this population requires careful discussion
  • Personal history of pancreatitis (whether active, recent, or recurrent) - the risk-benefit decision in patients with prior pancreatitis should be made explicitly and documented by your physician
  • Pregnancy or planned pregnancy - GLP-1 medications should be discontinued at least 2 months before planned conception. If you are considering pregnancy, inform your physician immediately.
  • Severe gastroparesis (delayed stomach emptying) - may be worsened by GLP-1 medications
  • Active or untreated eating disorders - particularly anorexia nervosa, ARFID, or bulimia nervosa. GLP-1 medications can reinforce restrictive eating patterns and are not appropriate without specialist input and close monitoring in these patients

Drug Interactions (Important)

GLP-1 medications delay gastric emptying, which can affect the absorption of oral medications.

  • If you take oral contraceptives, levothyroxine, oral antibiotics, or any other time-sensitive medication, inform your prescribing physician.
  • If you rely on oral contraceptives for birth control, discuss whether you need additional contraception during the dose-escalation phase or during significant vomiting/diarrhoea.

If in doubt, call us before taking any action. We would rather answer a question than manage a complication.

6. Injection Technique

GLP-1 medications (semaglutide and tirzepatide in their injectable forms) are administered subcutaneously - meaning just beneath the skin, not into muscle or vein.

Approved Injection Sites

  • Abdomen - at least 5 cm away from the navel
  • Upper thigh - outer surface
  • Upper arm - outer surface (may require assistance)

Site Rotation

Rotate injection sites with each weekly dose. Do not inject repeatedly into the same small area, as this can cause lipohypertrophy (lumpy scar tissue) that impairs absorption.

A simple rotation pattern: right abdomen → left abdomen → right thigh → left thigh → repeat. Or use whichever system you can remember consistently.

Step-by-Step Technique

  1. Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water
  2. Remove the pen from the refrigerator 30 minutes before injection to allow it to reach room temperature - this reduces discomfort
  3. Check the medication: it should be clear and colourless to slightly yellow; do not use if cloudy, discoloured, or containing particles
  4. Clean the injection site with an alcohol swab and allow it to dry completely
  5. Remove the pen cap and attach a new needle (if required by your pen type)
  6. Prime the pen if instructed in the product leaflet
  7. Pinch the skin gently between your thumb and index finger
  8. Insert the needle at a 90-degree angle with a smooth, confident motion
  9. Press and hold the injection button; maintain pressure for the count recommended in your pen's instructions (usually 5-10 seconds) to ensure full dose delivery
  10. Withdraw the needle and apply gentle pressure with a cotton ball - do not rub
  11. Dispose of the needle safely in a sharps container

Storage

  • Unopened pens: Store in the refrigerator (2-8°C). Do not freeze.
  • In-use pens: May be kept at room temperature (below 30°C) for up to the period specified in the product leaflet (typically 4-6 weeks depending on the medication)
  • Keep away from direct sunlight and heat
  • Do not use a pen past its expiry date

7. Protein - The Non-Negotiable Nutrient

This section is critical. Please read it carefully.

Why Protein Matters So Much on GLP-1 Medications

GLP-1 medications reduce appetite dramatically. This is the mechanism that drives weight loss - but it creates a significant risk that many patients and even some clinicians underestimate: you may lose substantial muscle mass alongside fat.

Research consistently shows that when caloric intake is severely restricted without adequate protein and resistance exercise, a significant proportion of weight lost comes from lean mass (muscle). Some studies on GLP-1 medication users have estimated that without intentional effort, roughly 25-40% of total weight lost may be lean tissue rather than fat - comparable to other methods of significant caloric restriction. Importantly, body composition ratios (the proportion of lean vs. fat mass) often improve during treatment because fat is lost at a greater rate. The concern is the absolute quantity of lean mass lost, especially in older patients or those with already-low muscle mass. Resistance training and adequate protein substantially reduce this risk (Blundell et al., Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 2021).

Why this matters:

  • Muscle mass is your primary metabolic engine - it determines how many calories you burn at rest
  • Loss of muscle makes weight regain after stopping medication more likely and more severe
  • Muscle loss in mid-life is associated with increased risk of falls, fractures, metabolic dysfunction, and cardiovascular disease
  • Rebuilding lost muscle is significantly harder than preserving it

Your Protein Target

Aim for 1.0-1.5 grams of protein per kilogram of your ideal body weight, daily. (ASMBS/Obesity Medicine 2025 consensus, NUT-002)

Important: This target is calculated using ideal body weight (IBW) - not your actual current weight. Using actual body weight in people with overweight or obesity overestimates protein needs significantly, sometimes by 30-100%. Your physician will provide your personalised IBW-based target at your consultation.

For example (using IBW):

  • If your ideal body weight is 70 kg → 70-105 g protein per day
  • If your ideal body weight is 85 kg → 85-128 g protein per day

Higher intakes (toward 1.5 g/kg IBW) are appropriate for very active individuals or those engaged in intensive resistance training. The 1.6+ g/kg figures sometimes cited come from sports medicine literature for highly trained athletes and are not the clinical standard for GLP-1 outpatients.

Practical Protein Sources (per 100g or typical serving)

  • Chicken breast (cooked): ~31g protein
  • Salmon (cooked): ~25g protein
  • Eggs: ~13g protein (2 large eggs ≈ 12-13g)
  • Greek yoghurt (plain, full-fat): ~10g per 100g
  • Cottage cheese: ~11g per 100g
  • Lentils (cooked): ~9g per 100g
  • Tofu (firm): ~8-15g per 100g depending on brand
  • Protein powder (whey or plant-based): typically 20-25g per scoop

Practical Tips When Appetite Is Suppressed

  • Lead with protein at every meal - eat protein first, then vegetables, then other foods
  • When you can only eat small amounts, make protein the priority
  • Liquid protein sources (Greek yoghurt, protein shakes, cottage cheese) are easier to consume when nausea is present
  • Do not skip meals entirely - even small, protein-focused meals matter
  • If you are consistently unable to meet protein targets due to appetite suppression, discuss this at your next clinic visit

8. Resistance Training - Essential, Not Optional

The Evidence Is Clear

In the context of GLP-1 medication use, resistance training is the single most important lifestyle intervention for preserving muscle mass and improving body composition outcomes.

Evidence from resistance training research consistently shows that those who train during caloric restriction preserve significantly more lean mass while achieving equivalent or greater fat loss (Lopez et al., Obesity Reviews, 2022 — meta-analysis of RCTs; Jensen et al., 2024 — exercise combined with GLP-1 for weight maintenance). Note: a dedicated large-scale RCT of resistance training specifically during semaglutide therapy is not yet published; the evidence base draws on exercise science and GLP-1 exercise sub-studies. The practical implication: the goal is not just a lower number on the scale — it is a healthier, stronger body with better metabolic function.

Minimum Effective Dose

2-3 resistance training sessions per week, each lasting 30-60 minutes.

This is the evidence-based minimum. Three sessions per week is preferable if your schedule and recovery allow it.

What Counts as Resistance Training

  • Weight machines at a gym
  • Free weights (dumbbells, barbells, kettlebells)
  • Resistance bands
  • Bodyweight exercises (push-ups, squats, lunges, planks)
  • Pilates (classical or reformer, with a resistance focus)

Aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) is valuable for cardiovascular health and is encouraged in addition to resistance training - but it does not adequately substitute for resistance work when muscle preservation is the goal.

How to Get Started

If you are new to resistance training or have been inactive:

  • Begin with 2 sessions per week of 30 minutes
  • Focus on compound movements that work multiple muscle groups: squats, deadlifts (or Romanian deadlifts), rows, presses, and lunges
  • Start with light weights and correct form before increasing load
  • Consider 2-3 sessions with a qualified personal trainer to learn safe technique - this is a worthwhile investment
  • Progress gradually: aim to increase weight or repetitions modestly each week

If you have joint problems or other physical limitations that make standard resistance training difficult, please discuss this with your physician. There are appropriate modified programmes for almost every situation. If you have a known cardiovascular condition (including heart failure, recent myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, or uncontrolled hypertension), please obtain clearance from your physician before starting a resistance training programme.

9. Hydration

Why Hydration Deserves Special Attention on GLP-1 Medications

When appetite is suppressed, thirst is often suppressed along with it. Many patients on GLP-1 medications drink significantly less than they realise. Combined with nausea and the possibility of vomiting, dehydration can develop quickly and worsen side effects considerably.

Dehydration is one of the most common and most preventable reasons patients experience worsened nausea, fatigue, constipation, and headaches during the early weeks of treatment.

Daily Targets

  • Minimum: 2.0-2.5 litres of water daily for most adults
  • In warm weather, during exercise, or if you are experiencing vomiting or diarrhoea, increase this further
  • Plain water is the best source; herbal teas, diluted electrolyte drinks, and clear broths also count
  • Caffeinated beverages (coffee, tea) count toward total fluid intake but have a mild diuretic effect - account for this if consuming large quantities

Signs of Dehydration - Act On These

Mild to moderate:

  • Dark yellow or amber urine (pale yellow is the target)
  • Urinating less than 3-4 times per day
  • Dry mouth or lips
  • Headache
  • Fatigue or difficulty concentrating

Severe - contact the clinic or seek emergency care:

  • Minimal or no urine output for more than 8 hours
  • Dizziness on standing
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Rapid heart rate with dizziness

Practical Tips

  • Keep a water bottle visible at all times - out of sight, out of mind applies to hydration
  • Set a reminder on your phone if needed - 250ml per hour across 8-10 waking hours is a simple target
  • Check your urine colour each morning: pale yellow = good, dark yellow = drink more
  • If nausea makes large volumes difficult, sip small amounts frequently rather than drinking in large quantities

10. Nutrition Beyond Protein

Total Caloric Intake - Don't Under-Eat

One of the counterintuitive risks of GLP-1 medications is eating too little. While appetite suppression is the mechanism of action, extreme caloric restriction (below approximately 1,000-1,200 kcal/day for most adults) accelerates muscle loss, causes nutrient deficiencies, and is not sustainable.

Aim for a moderate caloric deficit - typically 500-750 kcal below your maintenance needs - rather than near-starvation intake. Discuss your specific target with your physician or a registered dietitian.

Fibre

Aim for 25-35g of dietary fibre daily. Fibre:

  • Supports bowel regularity (critical for managing constipation)
  • Feeds beneficial gut bacteria
  • Slows glucose absorption and supports satiety

Good sources: vegetables (particularly leafy greens, broccoli, carrots), legumes (lentils, beans, chickpeas), whole grains (oats, quinoa), and seeds (chia, flaxseed).

Increase fibre gradually - adding large amounts quickly can worsen bloating, particularly in the early weeks.

Micronutrients

Eating less means consuming fewer micronutrients. Priority nutrients to monitor:

  • Vitamin D and calcium: Important for bone health, especially if dairy intake decreases. Supplement if your diet is insufficient - discuss with your physician.
  • B vitamins (particularly B12): At risk if animal product intake drops significantly
  • Iron and zinc: Important if red meat consumption decreases substantially
  • Magnesium: Often insufficient in modern diets; deficiency can contribute to constipation, muscle cramps, and poor sleep
  • Electrolytes (sodium, potassium): At risk during any period of vomiting or diarrhoea

A general-purpose multivitamin and vitamin D supplement are reasonable for most patients on GLP-1 medications, particularly during active weight loss. Discuss your specific needs with your physician.

Meal Timing and Structure

With reduced appetite, meal structure becomes more important - not less. Skipping meals entirely leads to protein deficits and energy crashes.

Recommended approach:

  • 3 smaller meals daily, each built around a protein source
  • An additional high-protein snack if needed to meet protein targets
  • Eat at consistent times - do not wait until you feel hungry, as the hunger signal may not reliably appear
  • Stop eating before you feel full - with slowed gastric emptying, fullness arrives significantly after the appropriate stopping point. Overeating leads to discomfort and nausea.

Foods to Limit During Adjustment

  • High-fat foods: Worsen nausea and delay gastric emptying further
  • Alcohol: Use with caution. In addition to worsening GI side effects, alcohol can mask symptoms of low blood sugar in patients on insulin or sulfonylureas and accelerates dehydration. Some patients notice a reduced desire for alcohol while on GLP-1 medications - this is a recognised effect and is not a cause for concern
  • Highly processed foods: Low nutrient density; poor value when total food volume is reduced
  • Carbonated beverages: Can worsen bloating and nausea

11. Stopping or Tapering the Medication

Do Not Stop Abruptly Without Physician Guidance

GLP-1 medications are not addictive, but they are actively suppressing appetite and supporting metabolic function as long as they are being taken. Stopping abruptly - without adequate lifestyle consolidation and physician oversight - carries a high risk of rapid weight regain.

When Stopping May Be Considered

Your physician will discuss this with you. Common scenarios include:

  • Pregnancy planning (medication should be discontinued before conception; discuss timing)
  • Significant adverse effects that cannot be managed
  • Achievement of weight and metabolic goals with solid lifestyle foundations established
  • Cost or access issues - always discuss alternatives before stopping

How Tapering Works

Dose reduction (rather than abrupt discontinuation) is generally preferred when stopping is planned. Your physician will guide this process based on your clinical situation. There is no universal tapering schedule - it is individualised.

During and after stopping, the lifestyle habits you have built - protein intake, resistance training, hydration, meal structure - become your primary tools. This is why those habits must be established during treatment, not deferred until after.

12. Weight Regain - The Honest Conversation

This section contains information that is uncomfortable but important. You deserve to know it.

The Evidence on Weight Regain

Multiple studies have examined what happens to patients who stop GLP-1 medications without maintaining lifestyle interventions. The results are consistent:

  • The STEP 1 Extension study (semaglutide): Patients who discontinued semaglutide regained, on average, approximately two-thirds of their lost weight within one year (Wilding et al., Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, 2022). Note: this is distinct from the STEP 4 trial (Rubino et al., JAMA, 2021), which showed +6.9% weight regain over 48 weeks in a withdrawal arm - a different study design with different findings.

This is not a personal failure - it reflects the biology of obesity. The hormonal drivers of weight gain (reduced GLP-1 sensitivity, elevated hunger hormones, altered reward signalling) reassert themselves when the medication is removed, unless counterbalanced by consolidated lifestyle habits.

What This Means for You

The medication is a tool, not a cure. For most patients, long-term treatment - either continuous or in a maintenance protocol - is necessary for sustained weight management, similar to how antihypertensives are used long-term for blood pressure.

Your physician will discuss the appropriate long-term strategy for your situation. This may include:

  • Continued treatment at the therapeutic dose
  • A reduced maintenance dose after goals are reached
  • A monitored period off medication with structured lifestyle support and clear re-initiation criteria
  • Regular monitoring of weight, metabolic markers, and wellbeing regardless of medication status

The lifestyle habits covered in this guide - protein, resistance training, hydration, meal structure - are your long-term insurance policy. Patients who consolidate these habits during treatment are significantly more likely to maintain their results.

13. Follow-Up Schedule and Contacting Élan Clinic

Standard Follow-Up Timeline

4 weeks after starting:

  • Review of side effects and tolerability
  • Dose adjustment assessment
  • Checking protein intake and exercise adherence
  • Any early questions or concerns

3 months:

  • Weight and body composition progress review
  • Bloodwork (fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids, liver enzymes, kidney function as indicated)
  • Dose optimisation
  • Nutrition and exercise review

6 months:

  • Comprehensive metabolic assessment
  • Evaluation of long-term strategy
  • Discussion of goals and adjustments

12 months:

  • Full review: clinical outcomes, lifestyle consolidation, long-term plan
  • Decision on continuation, dose adjustment, or transition to maintenance protocol

If you are progressing well and have no concerns, do not wait for scheduled appointments to share good news - we are glad to hear it. If you have concerns between appointments, contact us.

When to Contact Us Between Appointments

  • Any side effect that is worsening rather than improving after the first 2-4 weeks
  • Inability to meet protein targets consistently
  • Weight plateau lasting more than 4-6 weeks at a stable dose (may indicate dose adjustment needed)
  • New medications prescribed by another physician (some medications interact with GLP-1 agents)
  • Pregnancy or planning for pregnancy
  • Any of the red flags listed on page 1
